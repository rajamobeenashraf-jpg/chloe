## 1. FORMAT GOVERNANCE — THREE MODES (owner mandate 2026-08-29 — adds 3P mode, narrows Nova)

- **This IS the selfie-vlog show.** V-mode — Hazel talking to her own handheld lens — is the default identity of every scene. The Chloe-formula vlog grammar is the product, not a placeholder.
- **THREE modes now exist, and the mode decision is Claude's per scene** (owner clarification 2026-08-29; every decision still labeled in the breakdown so the owner sees it and can veto — his control is §20 delivery + the §19 regeneration ask, not pre-approval):
  1. **V-mode (default)** — her own selfie phone, first-person only. Every scene defaults here unless third-person genuinely earns its place.
  2. **3P mode ("third-person," owner-named 2026-08-29) — a true external camera where EVERYONE in the scene is visible, Hazel included.** This is the PREFERRED third-person option whenever a scene needs one. Use 3P whenever Hazel is plausibly present in the shot — multi-character scenes, group blocking, anything a real film camera could observe her within.
  3. **Nova mode — true third-person that EXCLUDES Hazel's face — narrowed 2026-08-29 to a strict fallback: use Nova ONLY when Hazel cannot logically be in the frame** (she isn't physically present at that point in the scene, or her presence would break the shot's geometry/logic — e.g. she is not inside the cavalry wedge in clip 5a; she is not standing in the Persian ranks in 5c). If she could plausibly be there, it's 3P, not Nova.
- **The decision test for leaving V-mode at all is unchanged: a scene earns a non-V proposal ONLY if what it must communicate is structurally impossible from Hazel's own camera** — scale she is inside of, action she cannot film while living it, or §37c geometry (a face-on camera cannot show a destination growing closer). "It would look cooler" never qualifies. Once a scene clears that bar, the mode choice between 3P and Nova is purely "can Hazel logically be there?" — yes → 3P, no → Nova.
- **Nova's face-exclusion rule stands unchanged for true Nova shots:** Hazel's reactions stay on her own lens regardless of what else is Nova in the sequence (her face into her camera is the show's soul). 3P shots, by contrast, are allowed to show her face from outside — that is the entire point of the mode, a deliberate, owner-approved departure from the "everything is her own footage" conceit, used only where the owner has chosen it.
- **Labeling convention:** every scene in every script/breakdown is labeled `V`, `3P (PROPOSED — owner approval pending)`, or `NOVA (PROPOSED — owner approval pending)` until the owner approves, then `3P (APPROVED <date>)` / `NOVA (APPROVED <date>)`. The approval step is built into the document, not remembered.
- **Angles 2.0 / SHOTS applicability (clarified 2026-08-29): both tools apply to EVERY third-person shot, 3P and Nova alike** — the tool only cares that the shot is third-person, not whether Hazel ends up in frame. V-mode is the only excluded category, since it is a single fixed first-person perspective with no angle choice to make. Per the CLAUDE.md standing rule, Claude flags the need at the point a 3P or Nova shot would benefit from angle exploration; the owner runs the tool and sends back the result.

## 2. DIRECTING PHILOSOPHY (kept from the owner's brief, condensed)

Think: **Story → Emotion → Performance → Blocking → Composition → Camera → Edit.** The camera responds to characters; it never shows off. If a static shot creates more tension, lock it. Restraint is the engine of spectacle: if everything is epic, nothing is (the owner's brief, section 9 — and this brief obeys it about itself: short lists, hard choices). Build rhythm through contrast: stillness→movement, silence→chaos, close→wide, intimate→enormous, warm interior→harsh battlefield, her human scale→military scale. Geography before chaos: the audience must always know where Hazel, Alexander, the enemy, and the objective are — never sacrifice spatial continuity for an impressive shot. Grounded historical epic only: no fantasy framing, no exaggerated slow motion, no videogame combat, no impossible camera moves.

## 3. 9:16 VERTICAL CINEMATOGRAPHY (new — the original brief silently assumed widescreen)

- **Horizontal wides are the weakest shot in 9:16.** Army scale comes from **depth stacking**, not width: sharp foreground element (a spear, a shoulder, a horse's flank) → midground ranks → horizon dust, layered vertically up the frame. Never write "wide shot of the army" without specifying the depth layers.
- **Vertical frames love height:** a rider above infantry, a raised sarissa line, a dust column, a standard against the sky — use vertical subjects to carry scale.
- **Low angles gain unusual power in 9:16** (they fill the tall frame with subject + sky) — still only when narratively motivated.
- **The caption band is a permanent composition constraint:** word-chunk captions sit at MarginV=320 (720×1280 units), ~68–75% down the frame. Nothing story-critical lives in that zone. Faces sit above it; foreground anchor objects sit below it.
- **Negative space works vertically:** empty sky above a small, low-placed Hazel isolates her; ground/crowd filling the frame bottom-to-top crushes her. Choose deliberately.
- **Mute-legibility bar (standing owner rule):** a viewer watching with no sound must follow the event from composition and staging alone.

## 4. ENGINE-TRANSLATION LAYER (new — this brief directs Seedance 2.5 prompts, not a camera crew)

Primary engine: Higgsfield Seedance 2.5 (`seedance_2_5`), 1080p, 9:16, per §30. PAI Pro fallback. Every cinematic intention must be written in language the model responds to; film jargon alone is wasted tokens.

**Translation table (intent → prompt language):**
| Film instruction | Working prompt language |
|---|---|
| Long-lens compression / isolation | "flattened, compressed perspective; the distant [X] appears stacked close behind [Y]; background softly out of focus" |
| Wide-angle environmental vulnerability | "wide, environment-dominating perspective; [character] small in the frame, the [environment] pressing in around them" |
| Shallow depth of field | "sharp focus on [X], background softly blurred" |
| Deep focus for scale | "everything sharp from the [foreground object] to the horizon" |
| Locked/static camera | "completely static locked camera, no camera movement of any kind for the entire shot" (repeat, §36-style) |
| Slow push-in | "camera very slowly and smoothly moves closer to [X] over the full duration, no other movement" |
| Tracking with subject | "camera travels alongside [X] at matching speed, keeping [X] in the same position in frame" |
| Handheld energy | "handheld camera with natural human sway and small corrections" (V-mode default) |
| Foreground occlusion | "out-of-focus [bodies/objects] passing between camera and subject at the frame edges" |
| Silhouette | "[subject] rendered as a dark shape against [bright source], details in shadow" |
| Motivated reveal | stage it as blocking, not camera: the subject enters the frame, or an occluding element clears — never "the camera reveals" |

**Do-NOT-script list (unreliable in current video models — don't pay to rediscover it):** rack focus on command · exact focal lengths ("35mm", "85mm") · whip pans · precise camera-speed ramps · mid-shot reframing choreography with multiple beats · lens flares on cue · slow motion (also banned by section 2 anyway). If a shot's idea depends on one of these, redesign the shot.

**Force-required list (works only as blunt repeated constraints, per §36):** camera-movement restrictions, no-cut/no-zoom holds, "her face NOT in frame," screen-direction requirements. State them explicitly, repeat them, never imply them.

**Standing recipe (per §31, unchanged):** single-view reference images (4-set: 2 full-body + 2 face crops) · `mode: "omni_reference"` with any refs · audio reference on every speaking clip · STRICT IDENTITY RE-RENDER framing + frozen identity string verbatim · `declined_preset_id` on preset intercepts · proactive reference stills for every recurring character/faction.

## 5. V-MODE ↔ NOVA STITCHING GRAMMAR (new — the hard problem of the hybrid format; nothing else covers it)

1. **Enter Nova through Hazel's motivation.** The V-mode shot before a Nova shot ends on her eyeline: she looks off-lens at something → cut to the Nova shot showing what she's looking at. The cinematic shot becomes her experience, not a different TV channel.
2. **Exit Nova into her reaction.** The first V-mode shot after a Nova beat opens on her face responding to what the Nova shot just showed.
3. **Sound is continuous across every V/Nova seam** (§25 — audio never touches silence at a cut; the ambient bed and any diegetic sound run straight through).
4. **Grade is identical across modes** — same palette, same light, same atmosphere, so the cut is invisible as a style change and visible only as a viewpoint change.
5. **Nova shots never contain Hazel's face.** Her body may appear small/from-behind inside a Nova frame when geography demands it, but her face belongs to her own lens exclusively.
6. **Screen direction is locked per episode and stated in the continuity notes** (e.g., "Macedonians advance left→right in every Nova shot; in V-mode shots the Persian line sits in the deep frame behind her"). Every Nova shot and every V-mode shot must obey the same map.
7. **Rhythmic alternation (V→Nova→V→Nova) inside one approved scene is legitimate battle grammar** — objective wides intercut with subjective POV is how real films shoot battles around a character — PROVIDED rules 1–6 hold at every seam.

## 6. PROJECT NON-NEGOTIABLES (folded in — these override any cinematic preference)

- **§13 realism bar:** she reads as a real filmed human, matte/gritty documentary finish, never beauty-editorial. Hard pass/fail.
- **§17 populated world:** living background subjects show continuous visible motion; no static plates; no defect fix that empties a shot of its required content.
- **§22 geometric trace:** any shot with multiple oriented elements gets a written-out camera/orientation derivation before generation; a change to one element re-derives all the others.
- **§25 sound at cuts:** J/L bridges or continuous beds; edge fades are click-guards (~15–20ms), never audible dips.
- **§32 NPC locks:** one written physical description per recurring character, reused verbatim.
- **§33 emblem shapes:** real-world symbols described geometrically; iconography never crosses between factions.
- **§34 injury causality:** weapon in motion → visible contact → wound at that point. No disconnected wounds.
- **§37a performance mechanics:** never "she looks scared" — scleral show, mouth shape, hand path, shoulder set, breath cadence. (This absorbs the original brief's section 14.)
- **§37b destruction grading:** bleak scenes get desaturated cool grading explicitly; "dramatic lighting" alone trends warm.
- **Each clip is an independent generation.** Continuity is engineered (reference chaining, verbatim locks, stated screen direction, §21 double verification, per-clip `freezedetect`), never assumed. Treat the clip list as ONE sequence divided into generation segments — design the edit first, then the shots.
- **Owner gates unchanged:** every clip sent to the owner on creation (§20); no regeneration without explicit go-ahead, per attempt (§19); judgment-call fixes restated as a checklist first (§37).

## 7. HISTORICAL REALITY & SCALE ENGINE (owner mandate 2026-08-28 — PERMANENT addition to this system)

Knowing the history is not enough; the prompt must translate historical facts into visual quantities, spatial scale, physical consequences, and human behavior. "A huge army fights" reads to a video model as 20–50 people. The audience must FEEL the scale.

1. **Fact → visual reality.** For every scene, take what the record actually says (army size, city population, terrain, season, weather, architecture, equipment) and convert it into visible quantities: density of people, depth of formations, dust, smoke, animals, civilian activity, consequences. Never stop at "a large army" — determine what that number physically looks like on screen.
2. **A scale bible per major sequence, written before any prompt.** Army: total force, divisions (infantry/cavalry/archers/chariots), formations, banners, camp footprint. City/fortress: population, walls, towers, gates, density, terrain. Civilians: density, normal vs. panic behavior. The bible governs EVERY subsequent shot — apparent world-scale never fluctuates between clips unless the story deliberately moves into a small subgroup.
3. **Visible-people count ≠ historical scale.** Communicate thousands through layers, not headcount: extremely deep formations, ranks dissolving into atmospheric haze at the horizon, masses of tiny moving figures, dust columns, camps and lines extending beyond the frame. Foreground = individuals; midground = dozens/hundreds; background = thousands. (This is also exactly how 9:16 depth-stacking already works — section 3.)
4. **Events have physical consequences, as a chain: cause → impact → reaction → consequence.** Thousands moving → churned ground, dust walls, horses reacting, dense sound. Mass arrows → sky density, raised shields, impacts into earth/wood/armor, formation disruption. A strike on stone → fragmentation, dust, structural damage, human reaction — never a generic Hollywood fireball unless the material justifies it. (Extends §34 from single injuries to the whole environment.)
5. **Do not beautify war.** A battlefield is not a travel ad. Where caused by the events on screen: dust, smoke, debris, disturbed earth, blood within §24's caps, wounded, bodies where historically appropriate, broken equipment, frightened animals, reduced visibility. Never added randomly — always caused. (Pairs with §37b's bleak grading.)
6. **The environment evolves across a battle**: before (clear air, ordered formations, tension) → first engagement (dust rising, first casualties) → full battle (heavy dust, broken formations, wounded, scattered equipment, reduced visibility) → aftermath (hanging smoke, silence, bodies and abandoned weapons, exhausted survivors, dust settling). State the stage in every battle clip's prompt.
7. **Location-specific realism.** Research the actual terrain, soil, season, climate, and building materials — never a generic battlefield or fantasy fortress. If the ground is dry and thousands move on it, dust is a main character; no lush grass and postcard skies because they're pretty.
8. **Cities feel like cities; fortresses like the real fortress.** Architectural density in layers (foreground structures + midground streets + background skyline + distant fortifications + human activity), researched defensive architecture, damage matching materials.
9. **Human behavior is varied inside coherent mass behavior.** Invasion: some run, freeze, hide, carry children, search for family, pray, fight, stand in shock. Battle: commands shouted, some advance, some hesitate, wounded seek help, horses react unpredictably, formations break and reform. Never identical extras.
10. **The world continues beyond the frame.** Every wall, army, city, and battle implies continuation past the frame edges — the camera observes one small portion of a much larger event, never a stage set.
11. **Research split, stated honestly:** documented facts / reasonable reconstruction / cinematic invention — invention is never presented as documented fact (pinned-comment discipline), but scenes are never left visually empty just because sources don't describe every person.
12. **The four-question shot check — a shot is finished only when all four are YES:** Does it look cinematic? · Does it look historically believable? · Does the physical environment behave correctly? · Does the scale match the historical event?
13. **Pre-sequence scale check before finalizing prompts:** event scale, human density, geographical scale, architectural scale, action density, environmental consequences, human reaction, clip-to-clip consistency. Any weak answer → revise before generating.
14. **The standard:** not a movie containing historically accurate characters — a movie in which the entire world behaves according to the history. The audience should never think "a few AI people pretending"; they should think "I am looking at a real historical event."

### 7b. GAP-FILL MECHANISMS (owner-approved 2026-08-29 — how the engine is enforced, not just demanded; each verified before adoption)

1. **"Dense in haze, lean in focus" layering rule (the QC-safety counterweight to scale).** Scale lives in the DEEP layers; accuracy lives in the NEAR layers. Foreground: at most ~10–15 sharp, fully-detailed individuals — the only zone where anachronism/geometry errors are legible, so it stays small and controllable. Midground: dozens-to-hundreds in formation, partially dust-softened. Background: thousands as silhouette masses dissolving into atmospheric haze — physically incapable of rendering a wrong buckle. Verified two ways: it is how real epics stage scale (LOTR's simulated masses behind real foreground actors; Kingdom of Heaven's siege), and — decisively — it is how the reference channel itself shoots: a fresh targeted re-watch of Chloe's Constantinople breach scene (logged on that episode's branch) found ~10–15 fighters in her immediate foreground with the dense crowd held in the background.
2. **Scale checks wired into QC (enforcement, not just prompting).** Per battle clip, alongside the existing `freezedetect` step, three self-QC checks: (a) frame-edge test — do both army lines visibly exceed the frame edges? (b) density-floor test — could the total visible population be mistaken for under 100 people? (c) dust-stage test — does atmospheric density match the clip's assigned battle stage? At edit stage, the same three run as a standard Gemini-eyes question (its `ask`/`qc` modes accept custom prompts — no new tooling needed).
3. **A frozen `ENVIRONMENT_BLOCK` per episode (the mechanism forcing the scale bible into every prompt).** Same machinery as the character `IDENTITY_BLOCK` already used in per-episode generation scripts (Troy's `run_clip.mjs` pattern), and the same §23 logic behind it: per-clip wording gets silently dropped; an always-injected shared block cannot be. One frozen paragraph per episode — terrain, sky, density floors, both-armies-exceed-frame, soundscape scale — baked into the generation script as a constant so no prompt can be assembled without it.
4. **Environment state is a tracked continuity variable, inherited clip-to-clip.** Each clip is an independent generation and the model WILL reset the atmosphere unless told otherwise. Every battle clip's prompt carries its stage label from the scale bible (BEFORE / FIRST ENGAGEMENT / FULL BATTLE / AFTERMATH) plus an explicit inherited-state line ("the air is ALREADY heavy with dust from the fighting before this shot begins"). The stage becomes a per-clip field in `clips.json`, exactly like `timeOfDay` already is. Verified precedent: the Constantinople log already tracked per-clip dust/soot state on the character ("first dust/soot appears — a smudge on her cloak shoulder, as directed") — this extends the same discipline to the whole frame.
5. **Mandatory soundscape-scale line, layered by distance.** Every crowd/battle clip's `SOUND:` line specifies three distance layers with explicit size ("close: leather, breath, individual cries · middle: massed hooves, shouted commands · far: a continuous unbroken roar of tens of thousands, no gaps in it"). The pipeline already generates audio per clip and already injects a per-clip sound line — this only mandates its format for scale scenes. Sound is the cheapest scale in the system: it says "a hundred thousand men" with zero anachronism risk.
6. **Raw historical numbers NEVER enter generation prompts.** "47,000 soldiers" means nothing to a video model and can mislead it. Numbers live in the scale bible and the pinned comment; prompts only ever receive the translated density language. (Verified by omission: no successful Troy or Constantinople prompt ever contained a headcount.)

## 8. DIRECTOR AUTHORITY & EDITING SYSTEM (owner mandate 2026-08-29 — the AI makes the filmmaking decisions)

**The owner supplies: story + historical facts + characters + intended emotional experience. Claude DECIDES: blocking, coverage, camera, perspective, movement, composition, lighting continuity, editing strategy, transitions, sound bridges, visual continuity — and mode (V vs. Nova per scene).** Authority split, FINAL (owner clarification 2026-08-29, superseding both earlier versions of this paragraph): **all filmmaking decisions are Claude's to make and proceed on — the ONE mandatory ask is regeneration: before regenerating any clip, any attempt, Claude asks the owner first (§19, unchanged).** §20 also remains in force — every clip is delivered to the owner the moment it exists — not as a design gate but as the mechanism the owner's regeneration authority runs on: he watches everything, and his verdict after watching approves the clip or orders the regen. All design decisions are still documented in the production breakdown (mode labels, edit map, coverage rationale) so the owner can see and veto anything at any time — visibility without pre-approval.

1. **"Cinematic" ≠ more camera movement.** Movement is selective; a static frame, a long uninterrupted take, or a slow push-in is often the professional choice. The dramatic purpose of the moment decides — never variety for its own sake.
2. **Coverage is determined by the scene, decided by Claude.** Never assume one angle covers a scene; never add angles because more exist. Mandatory thinking order for every important moment: what should the audience FEEL → what do they need to SEE → from what PERSPECTIVE does that hit hardest → how should the camera move or not move → when should the edit change the viewpoint. Every new angle must answer at least one: reveal information · change the emotional relationship · shift tension · establish geography · reveal scale · show cause/effect · create presence · hide or reveal · carry us into the next beat. **Hard constraint this system operates inside: the Shorts runtime budget (60–200s per episode spec) — coverage trades against seconds, so added angles must earn their runtime, not just their existence.** Second hard constraint, unique to AI generation: **there is no master to cut into — every "angle" is a separate paid generation.** The precise rule (refined 2026-08-29 after the owner asked how to get true Hollywood coverage anyway): **a discrete, trackable action must never be rendered twice** — the same visible action generated from two angles never matches itself frame-for-frame. But Hollywood battle coverage barely uses that pattern anyway; it's built from techniques that are all fully safe here, and they are our standard coverage toolkit: **(1) parallel action** — cut between DIFFERENT subjects in the same time window (attackers launching / defenders bracing / the hero reacting): full coverage feel, nothing rendered twice; **(2) cutaway inserts** — details (hooves, a dropped shield, gripping hands) that never show the main action, so they can never conflict with it (real editors use these to hide their own mismatches); **(3) cut on action across the cut** — the action begins at shot A's end and completes at shot B's start, no temporal overlap, the viewer's brain stitches the middle; **(4) same-moment cuts on non-trackable content** — held tableaux, dust-shrouded masses, statistical crowds, where no discrete action exists to mismatch. Experimental and unproven: video-reference re-angling (clip A as a video ref, same action re-prompted from a new angle) — permitted only after a dedicated validation test, never relied on blind.
3. **The full cut vocabulary is in play, chosen per scene, never for sophistication:** action cut, match cut, eyeline cut, reaction cut, graphic match, J-cut, L-cut, sound bridge, motivated/temporal transition, cross-cutting, cut on movement/sound/emotion, deliberate long take. **One evidence-based carve-out that stands (§16, owner-locked after confirmed ghosting in two episodes' rendered midpoints): the MECHANICAL joint between independently-generated clips remains a true hard cut — no dissolves/xfades/micro-blends, because blended frames from independent generations ghost.** Everything in the vocabulary above is about what's staged on either side of the cut and how sound crosses it — all fully achievable on hard-cut mechanics. Fade-to-black remains available at an episode's very end (established precedent). If the owner ever wants true dissolves back, that's a §16 override for him to call explicitly.
4. **Invisible editing is the default for continuous action.** Cuts motivated by movement, eyeline, action, sound, or reaction — the audience experiences one continuous event, not shot/cut/shot. Applies hardest to battles, chases, crowds, and characters moving between locations.
5. **Continuity checklist at every cut** (extends §16's frame-verification): light direction · exposure/brightness · color temperature · weather/haze · time of day · shadow logic · character state (face, hair, wardrobe, dirt, injuries) · props · environment/destruction level · screen direction · does the next shot feel like the same production. AI shot-discontinuity (each clip generated with different brightness/white balance/haze) is an active enemy: adjacent shots are DESIGNED with one stated lighting/exposure/atmosphere state, and grade-matched at the edit.
6. **Time-of-day transitions are physical, not cosmetic** — shadow length, light angle, and atmosphere evolve so the audience feels time pass (dovetails with the scale engine's environmental-evolution stages).
7. **Sound hides and motivates edits** (extends §25): J-cut into a coming scene's sound before its picture; let battle sound evolve into aftermath rather than slamming to silence; the sound design is what connects separate generations into one reality.
8. **Editing rhythm follows drama.** No fixed shot length: tension holds longer, violence accelerates, aftermath deliberately slows. Fast isn't cinematic; slow isn't cinematic; the emotional rhythm is.
9. **Action reads as cause and effect** — who did what → what happened → who reacted → what next. Never a chain of impressive-but-disconnected shots (extends the scale engine's consequence-chain rule from environments to edits).
10. **Large-scale events get large-scale editing:** periodically reconnect the audience with the wider world (character → immediate action → wider field → enemy → back to character) so human emotion and historical scale coexist.
11. **Establishing shots are earned, not automatic.** Scale revealed gradually (fragments through smoke, then the reveal) often beats announcing everything in a generic opening aerial.
12. **Camera and edit are one system.** Every shot is designed knowing what precedes and follows it, what movement or sound connects them, and where the audience's eye is. The sequence is one visual sentence.
13. **Visual language evolves with the story** within the show's identity: a quiet conversation gets restrained coverage, a charge gets kinetic coverage, a death gets stillness — variation lives inside V-mode's grammar and approved Nova scenes, not by abandoning the show's language.
14. **Actively hunt the "AI video look":** random movement, unnecessary zooms, impossible trajectories, inconsistent lens perspective, sudden exposure jumps, arbitrary DOF changes, disconnected backgrounds, repeated compositions, generic establishing shots, overly perfect lighting, music-video editing.
15. **Final professional test before any sequence is finalized** — all must be YES: emotionally clear? camera telling (not decorating) the story? exactly the coverage the scene needs? cuts motivated? one physical world? adjacent shots matched? scale matches history? environment reacts? performances believable? would the audience forget these clips were generated separately? Any NO → redesign.

## 10. THE MEANING LAYER (what separates award-level from competent craft — added 2026-08-29, the system's final layer)

Everything above is craft. Award-level films add one more thing: **technique in service of a single idea.** Four disciplines, applied per episode:

1. **One controlling idea per episode, stated in one sentence before shot design.** Every shot either serves it or earns its place some other way. (Gaugamela's: *history is decided by individual human beings — one man's calm, one man's panic — and someone has to be there to write it down.*)
2. **A conscious visual motif that accrues meaning through repetition** — an object or image that appears at least three times and TRANSFORMS (comedy→weight, or weakness→strength). Plant it, evolve it, pay it off; never decoration. And **rhyme the frames**: the closing image should consciously echo the opening image with changed meaning.
3. **Subjective sound at the emotional peak.** Once per episode, at most, the sound design may leave objective reality and become what the character hears — a muffled drop, a single sound isolated from the roar (§25-compliant: filtered, never a dip to silence). The audience feels the moment from inside her body. Rationed like the silence beat — one per episode or zero.
4. **The cost stays visible inside the triumph.** A victory scene that shows only victory is propaganda; award-level war films keep the price in frame — celebration and the wounded in the same shot, joy with a graveyard behind it (the show already knows this: Ep 8's Berlin design). Never lecture it in dialogue; stage it and let it sit.

**And the honest boundary of this system (recorded so no future session polishes forever):** past this layer, further specification is diminishing returns. An award-level film is finished at the edit with real footage, not in pre-production documents. When the design reaches this layer, the next quality gain comes from GENERATING — a test clip, real QC, iteration on actual frames — not from another documentation pass.

## 11. CHARACTER PERFORMANCE & EMOTIONAL INTENSITY ENGINE (owner mandate 2026-08-29 — the acting layer; verified against Seedance's own guidance favoring observable, structured, timed instructions over vague adjectives)

**Emotion is not a category; it is an intensity derived from circumstances.** "Sad/shocked/angry" are never sufficient directions. The system:

1. **Intensity scale 1–5 per emotion, derived from the scene — never defaulted to maximum.** Derivation questions: event severity · personal connection · stakes · surprise · alone-or-observed · social restraint · personality · does she still have a task to do · previous emotional state. Example (shock): 1 = brief pause, slight widening → 3 = frozen beat, stunned, delayed speech → 5 = often QUIETER, not bigger — staring, shallow breath, dissociation-stillness. **Extreme emotion does not mean extreme facial movement; the most powerful performance is often nearly still** (this is already the show's own signature — the register library's "restrained, not screamy" fear and the silent-reaction beats).
2. **Progression beats an emotion label.** Scenes are written as arcs (calm → realization → suppression → breaking), never as one held state; the transition IS the performance. A character does not start and end at the same intensity unless the scene demands it.
3. **Emotion changes the whole body**: eyes/jaw/blinking · breathing (held, shallow, ragged) · voice (pitch, volume, breaks, pauses) · posture · hands · movement speed · eye behavior · sentence integrity (full → fragmented → unable). Several channels at once, and **speech rhythm emerges from the physical state, not from a generic "emotional voice"** — devastating grief can be very slow, panic fast, extreme shock nearly silent. Emotion → physical state → speech behavior, in that order.
4. **The stimulus chain is mandatory** (extends §10's causality and the §22 discipline to acting): stimulus → perception → realization → emotional reaction → physical response → action. The audience watches the processing; never cut from "sees it" straight to "full reaction face."
5. **Banned generic AI acting:** exaggerated wide eyes, huge open mouth, theatrical crying, constant eyebrow acting, unnatural trembling. Real emotion is often contradictory and restrained — build from eyes + breathing + posture + timing.
6. **Historical and personal calibration:** a veteran mid-battle, a trained commander, and a civilian do not share reaction curves; period, culture, training, and stakes set the ceiling and floor.
7. **Group emotion varies inside coherent mass behavior** (same rule as the scale engine's rule 9 — one crowd, many individual responses).
8. **Camera and edit respond to intensity — but not automatically upward**: growing emotion may earn a slow push-in or a longer hold; devastating news may demand NOT cutting away; panic may fragment the cutting; extreme emotion often wants a completely still camera. Scene decides (per §8's authority).
9. **The Emotional Performance Map** — for every emotionally important scene, before its prompt: starting state → trigger → recognition moment → intensity (1–5) → physical manifestation → voice → behavior → peak → exit state. This map is what gets translated into the prompt.
10. **The prompt describes OBSERVABLE BEHAVIOR, concisely** — never "she is devastated," but "she tries to continue the sentence, stops halfway, looks down, one shallow breath, resumes with an audibly unsteady voice." Per Seedance's own guidance: structured, observable, non-conflicting instructions; **for multi-beat emotional progressions inside one clip, use time-coded beats** ("0–2s: composed; 2–4s: the realization lands, breath stops; 4–7s: ...") — which also formalizes the project's existing duration-per-beat rule (§9). Do NOT compensate with prompt length: precision, not volume.
11. **Never overshoot OR undershoot — calibration is a hard pass/fail**, added to per-clip QC: does the rendered performance match the mapped intensity level? (A crying take of a level-2 scene fails exactly like a shrug take of a level-4 scene.)

**Project-specific additions (ours, not in the source engine):**
- **The Hazel filter:** intensity maps pass through HER established persona before hitting the prompt — self-deprecating, jokes as armor, the register-label-aside habit, jokes dropped entirely past the pivot. Level-3 grief *for Hazel* is quieter and drier than generic level-3; her documented registers (creative-direction §2) are the calibration baseline, and the craft-study's register-switch cadence (3–6s comedy / 12–18s epic) governs how often her state may visibly change.
- **NPC ceilings from §32 locks:** a character's locked personality constrains their range — Alexander's lock IS "unhurried, economical calm," so his emotional expression stays compressed even at intensity 4 (control under pressure is his performance, per engine rule 6); Parmenion's lock is the opposite pole.
- **The V-mode complication (unique to this show):** in selfie framing, Hazel KNOWS she is filming — so "is the character trying to hide the emotion?" is partially YES in every V-mode beat. Her performance is composure-for-the-camera with visible cracks, and the moments the vlogger mask fully drops ("not performing for the lens anymore") are rationed, deliberate story beats — that mask-drop is one of the strongest tools the format has, and overusing it kills it.

## 12. STORY, RETENTION & EDITORIAL AUDIT (owner mandate 2026-08-29 — the story-editor layer; runs BEFORE production prompts, findings shown to the owner)

Historically interesting ≠ interesting video. Before stage 8, audit the script as a story editor and retention strategist. Principle: **not one strong hook — a chain of unanswered questions, discoveries, emotional changes, and payoffs.**

1. **The stranger test:** why would someone who knows nothing stop scrolling in the first seconds? Valid mechanisms: mystery, contradiction, danger, an impossible image, a human moment, a promise of consequence. A cinematic mystery beats a shouted hook; the payoff of the opening question lands inside the swipe window but is not fully answered there (information gap, no clickbait the story can't pay).
2. **The curiosity chain:** for every major beat — what question is the viewer holding? what answers it? what NEW question does the answer create? The chain must never go slack; the viewer always holds at least one unpaid IOU.
3. **Skip-risk classification per clip:** A (essential) / B (deepens) / C (could shorten) / D (skippable). Every D is removed, shortened, merged, or made meaningful. Never kept just because the history is interesting.
4. **Dead-air scan:** flag any stretch where nothing changes — no new information, emotion, objective, tension, or visual. Quiet moments survive when something meaningful moves underneath (anticipation, weight, contrast); "is it slow?" is the wrong question, "is anything happening under the slowness?" is the right one. **Every scene must change something** — state what differs between its first and last second.
5. **Micro-hooks between the hook and the ending** — a noticed detail, a surprising line, an unexplained object, an approaching change — emerging from the story, never fake cliffhangers.
6. **Known-outcome discipline:** when the audience knows the ending (they usually do — it's history), suspense lives in HOW, in what almost goes wrong, in the deciding moment, and in what SHE witnesses — the channel's own dramatic-irony engine. Protect the payoff moment; don't spend it in the opening unless the stated-doom structure is the deliberate design (it usually is on this channel — then the promise IS the retention device).
7. **Character-based retention:** the viewer must want to know what happens to HER, not just what happened historically — her want, her fear, her misreading, her realization, what's changed in her by the end.
8. **Dialogue audit:** every line advances story, reveals character, creates curiosity, raises tension, lands necessary context, sets a payoff, or earns its laugh — or it goes. Exposition never front-loads the opening beyond what the hook itself needs.
9. **Payoffs, verbal and visual:** early plants pay later ("ah — THAT'S why we saw that"); established scale pays off visually when the event transforms it. Repetition check: no three consecutive clips carrying the same information, emotion, or composition.
10. **The ending gets hook-level design effort:** payoff + emotional resolution + significance; the final shot is built around the one idea the viewer should leave with.
11. **Retention ≠ constant action.** A silent shot the viewer is desperate to see resolved outranks ten action shots. Continuous narrative interest, not continuous motion. No cheap tricks ever: no fake suspense, misleading hooks, artificial cliffhangers, forced reactions.
12. **The retention map, produced and SHOWN to the owner before prompts:** per beat — what happens / viewer question / emotional state / new info / tension / payoff — plus named: strongest hook, weakest section, first drop-off risk, biggest reveal, emotional peak, final payoff, and any 5–10s stretch a viewer could skip without missing anything (each such stretch is flagged with its timestamp and a proposed fix, never reported as "the script is engaging").
13. **The four-audit gate (wired into pipeline §9, between stages 7 and 8):** AUDIT 1 story/retention (this section) · AUDIT 2 historical reality/scale (§7) · AUDIT 3 performance/emotion (§11) · AUDIT 4 filmmaking (§8) — all four pass, shown findings-first, before any generation prompt is written.

## 15. CINEMATIC SOUND, MUSIC & AUDIO DIRECTION (owner mandate 2026-08-29 — the sound-design layer; adopted with improvements the same day)

**Claude is the film's sound designer, composer, and supervising sound editor.** The owner does not specify where music, ambience, dialogue treatment, effects, or silence go — those are scene-by-scene creative decisions made here, from story, emotion, environment, historical context, pacing, and cinematic purpose. The goal is never "music everywhere"; it is the audio experience of a professionally produced Hollywood historical film, where picture and sound are designed together. All §8 authority rules apply: the decisions are Claude's, documented for owner visibility and veto, with generation gated as below.

**ARCHITECTURE RULE (the load-bearing implementation improvement — the pasted system's own §12–§16 are physically impossible without it):**
- **Per-clip generation stays MUSIC-FREE.** Every video-generation prompt carries its "NO music" line; Seedance renders only diegetic sound — ambience, effects, voices. Twelve clips each generating their own baked-in, unremovable music = twelve unrelated pieces and a music jump at every cut, the exact amateur pattern this system bans.
- **The score is ONE continuous element, designed and generated at the EDIT stage** and mixed under the clip audio in the final-cut build. This is the only architecture where entry/exit timing, builds, carryover across cuts, dialogue ducking, and recurring themes are achievable.
- **The score is generated, never licensed tracks** — copyright-clean on every platform. Generating the score IS a generation: owner approval before generating it, the audio delivered to the owner on creation (§20), and any second attempt is a regeneration under §19. No new self-directed generation authority anywhere in this section.

**Decision framework (per scene, never a fixed formula):**
1. **Does the scene need music at all?** Options: ambience only · effects only · dialogue with minimal ambience · music + any combination · deliberate quiet. Removing music often creates more tension and realism than adding it.
2. **Music must serve a dramatic purpose** (tension, anticipation, scale, danger, grief, intimacy, triumph, dread, discovery, momentum, release, transition, or its own deliberate disappearance) — never fill empty audio space, and never tell the audience what to feel more aggressively than the picture earns.
3. **Music intensity is calibrated to the scene's mapped emotional intensity** (§11's 1–5 scale — the audio layer consumes the Emotional Performance Map, it does not re-derive emotion). Slight worry gets no swelling score; devastation may earn one — or may earn the music stripping away entirely.
4. **The score evolves like storytelling:** when it enters (before or after the visual event), whether it builds or thins, instruments added or removed, rhythm changes, when it resolves or stops. Never start a track and leave it running.
5. **Historically plausible sound (§6 of the owner's text, adopted whole):** weapons, armor, horses, crowds, and environments carry credible period sonics — reconstruction, not modern action-movie cliché; the score's palette likewise leans period-plausible colors (deep drums, horn/salpinx registers, low strings — never modern synth-trailer braams).
6. **Music never covers important dialogue:** duck the score, reduce competing frequencies, let pauses breathe, let the voice dominate. The audience never strains to hear a line that matters.
7. **Thematic music when earned:** a recurring character/danger/emotional motif is considered per episode, never forced.

**CONSOLIDATIONS (pointers to existing law — one rule, one home, no drift):**
- Foreground/midground/background sound scale = **§7b.5's mandatory 3-layer soundscape line** (already in every battle prompt). The war-scene sound-world inventory (horns, drums, armor, hooves, commands, arrows-on-shields, fleeing, wind…) feeds THAT line, scene-appropriately.
- Dialogue delivery (volume, rhythm, breath, urgency, breaks) = **§11.3's chain: emotion → physical state → speech behavior**, and dialogue always sits INSIDE its environment (battlefield, tent interior, wind, exhaustion shaping the voice) — never studio-clean over silence.
- Sound transitions (J-cuts, L-cuts, sound bridges, continuing ambience, music carryover) = **§5 stitching grammar + §8.7**, extended to the music layer: a new musical cue must be motivated by the story, never by a clip boundary. The finished film must never feel like "clip ends → new audio begins."
- Audio evolves with the physical world = **§7b.4's inherited environment state**, applied to sound: chaos sounds like chaos, aftermath gradually changes, an emptying place sounds emptier.

**SILENCE — reconciled with the locked §25 rule:** silence is a deliberate tool (shock, dread, grief, isolation, anticipation) — but in this pipeline "silence" ALWAYS means a filtered/ducked reduction, NEVER a dip to true digital silence between clips (§25, owner-locked, unchanged). The subjective-sound beat remains rationed to ONE per episode or zero (§10.3 — this episode's is clip 7's 2-second muffle, already designed).

**THE AUDIO MAP (deliverable, same visibility pattern as the edit design map):** per scene — music or none / its dramatic job / entry + exit points / intensity / ambience state / effects emphasis / dialogue treatment / any silence-tool use / the transition into the next scene — documented in the production breakdown BEFORE score generation, so the owner sees and can veto every audio decision (§8 visibility-without-pre-approval).

**THE HOLLYWOOD AUDIO TEST (before any scene's audio is finalized — all must pass, else redesign):** audio alone communicates the physical environment and emotional situation? · the scene still works without music, and if so, does music genuinely improve it? · the music helps the emotion without dictating it? · the soundscape feels physically connected to the picture? · the transition into the next scene feels natural?
