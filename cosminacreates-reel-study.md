## THE core finding — why her openings work and ours doesn't

**In all six reels, the HISTORICAL FIGURE speaks first, to HER, in the very first
second — the video opens in the middle of an interaction, usually a confrontation.**
There is never a solo walking-and-narrating intro. The premise ("Visiting Pablo
Escobar in 1987"-style) is carried by a static top title banner, which frees the
opening dialogue from having to explain anything. Three ingredients, every time:

1. **In media res + stakes:** she has already been caught / grabbed / challenged by
   one of history's most dangerous people. The viewer's first thought is "is she in
   trouble?" — not "what is this video about?"
2. **The figure's authority vs. her modern casualness** — the tension/comedy engine
   ("Someone who's, um, not remotely scared of you, if that helps").
3. **The banner does the exposition**, so second 1 can be drama instead of setup.

Our current Escobar clip 1 (Hazel walks under the gate narrating facts alone) is
precisely the intro she never shoots.

## The other seven transferable mechanics

1. **Dual role, hard-switched:** in-scene guest (talks TO the figure) ↔ fourth-wall
   narrator (turns to lens, delivers the fact the figure must not hear). We already
   have this; she switches more often — nearly every 10–20s.
2. **Every fact is attached to a prop, action, or visual** — never recited into
   air. Sugar candy → blackened-teeth close-up; half-painted face → smallpox + lead
   makeup; hidden locket ring → the executed mother; portraits on a wall → the
   rivals; map on a table; bandaged leg → the ulcer. Fact delivery IS scene
   business.
3. **Question-driven chapters:** each 15–25s segment opens with her direct question
   ("Can we talk about Francis?" / "Were you ever actually athletic?"), which
   resets curiosity like a mini-hook. Chapters escalate: fun/gross-out trivia →
   personal trauma → the hardest question (saved for late) → legacy.
4. **The figure gets a written, quotable closing monologue** — the emotional payoff
   viewers quote in comments ("I was not only the thing I did"; "Ask any man who
   ruled beside a wife whether he'd have traded places with me").
5. **The figure notices HER strangeness** — the premise reflected back in-world:
   Anne Boleyn: "You have looked at me all day like a woman reading a book she has
   already finished." Devastating, literal, plain — and it makes the time-travel
   conceit pay emotionally.
6. **Countdown irony stated as plain numbers:** "May 1536. Anne has 15 days left.
   Nobody in this room has told her." Dates and day-counts, not vague doom.
7. **Named-villain specificity in crowds:** "This one testifies against her. This
   one signs the warrant. This one was cheering at her coronation." Pointing at
   individuals beats describing a mood.

Production style notes: shot/idea changes every 2–10s; word-synced 1–3-word
captions (we already match); score shifts per chapter (comedic → somber → swelling
strings at the close); high-immersion period sets; her physical touch (holding
hands, wiping tears) collapses the 500-year distance.

## What we should NOT copy

- Her runtimes (128–166s) justify slower single-topic pacing; our episode is
  denser.
- Two of six reels are pure tragedy with zero humor — that fits doomed queens, not
  our channel's default register.
- Her captions include a persistent top premise banner — ADOPTING IT IS A PROPOSAL
  to the owner (our locked caption system doesn't currently include one), not a
  decision.

linear day preserved: confronted at arrival → welcomed → tour → lunch...):**

  per mechanic #2; the gate-plane fact moves to the walk-out under the plane
